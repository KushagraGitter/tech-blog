Elon Musk Says a $3.5 Trillion SpaceX-Tesla Merger Has "A Lot of Merit" — and Now There Are Committees to Prove It

October 21, 2026 · Kushagra · 8 min read

For three years, "will SpaceX buy Tesla" has been the kind of question Elon Musk answered with a shrug and a joke. On Tuesday's SpaceX third-quarter earnings call, the shrug disappeared. Asked directly about a merger, Musk said he was "tap dancing through a minefield" — then added, without much hedging, that he thinks combining the two companies "would be a good idea." Within minutes, SpaceX shares were up more than 5% in after-hours trading and Tesla added roughly 1%. By the next trading session, financial media had already done the arithmetic: a combined company would be worth about $3.5 trillion, with SpaceX valued near $2 trillion and Tesla near $1.5 trillion, according to Fortune's reporting on Tuesday's closing prices.

What actually happened on the call

The news wasn't a deal — it was the disclosure that a deal is now being studied through a formal process. Musk told analysts that both companies have quietly formed special committees made up of independent directors "at the request of shareholders" to discuss a potential combination. Because Musk runs both companies and holds large stakes in each, he said he's legally a "conflicted party" and is shut out of the committees' actual deliberations: "It's an odd kabuki dance where two groups of people are talking and I can't be in the conversation."

Crucially, Musk was careful to lower expectations in the same breath he raised them. "Some people out there assume that a SpaceX and Tesla merger is a certainty, and I think that would be overstating it," he said, per Fortune's transcript of the call. The committees haven't agreed on any terms, there's no proposal on the table, and Tesla's own annual shareholder proxy — filed just one day earlier, on Monday — contains no merger item for a vote. Any real transaction would need separate approval from a majority of all outstanding Tesla shares, not just votes cast, a bar Musk himself called "quite a high hurdle," per CNN's coverage.

The ownership math that makes this complicated

The reason this story is more than earnings-call color is Musk's lopsided control of the two companies. He holds roughly 20% of Tesla's equity but only a modest share of its voting power in relative terms, while at SpaceX he owns about 45-48% of the equity and controls more than 82% of the voting power through super-voting shares, according to regulatory filings cited by Ars Technica and Fortune. A merger structured the wrong way could meaningfully increase Musk's effective control over the combined entity — which is exactly the kind of structure that invites shareholder lawsuits, proxy advisor objections, and regulatory scrutiny long before any deal closes.

There's also fresh financial entanglement to account for: Tesla's Monday proxy statement confirmed a $2 billion investment by Tesla into SpaceX, on top of the Terafab chip-manufacturing complex the two companies are already jointly developing. Musk framed the strategic logic around that kind of convergence — AI software, chip design, robotics, and orbital connectivity — telling investors "we are pretty much in the superintelligence singularity" and that cooperation across the companies would accelerate all of it, according to Ars Technica's transcript.

Why this is landing differently than past merger chatter

Speculation about a SpaceX-Tesla tie-up isn't new — reporters were asking Musk about it as far back as SpaceX's blockbuster IPO in July, when he deflected entirely, saying the topic couldn't even be discussed properly on an earnings call. What changed this week is specificity and sequencing. SpaceX just posted its first quarterly profit since going public — net income of $4.4 billion for the July-to-September period, reversing a $1.1 billion loss a year earlier, driven partly by renting out compute capacity to AI firms. CFO Bret Johnsen reaffirmed a target of $100 billion in annualized revenue by the end of this year, and Musk projected SpaceX's annual recurring revenue could reach $500 billion to $1 trillion by the end of 2027 — numbers that, if anywhere close to accurate, would make SpaceX one of the most valuable private companies ever to approach a public combination.

That profitability gives the merger conversation a concrete anchor it never had before: this is no longer "could Musk's companies combine someday," it's "two profitable, multi-hundred-billion-dollar companies have empaneled independent directors to work out whether and how." SpaceX also already owns xAI, which it folded in back in February, meaning a Tesla combination would pull robotics (Optimus), EVs and energy storage, rockets and Starlink/Starmind connectivity, and frontier AI all under one corporate roof — a vertical stack no other company on Earth can claim.

The obstacles nobody is glossing over

Valuation disagreement is the first landmine. Morningstar has previously flagged that reconciling SpaceX's private-market valuation against Tesla's volatile public one will likely be contentious, particularly for Tesla shareholders who could see their stake diluted into a combined entity dominated by Musk's SpaceX holdings. Historically, Tesla's shareholder base has rubber-stamped Musk's biggest asks — including his record-breaking pay package — but a full corporate merger that could be read as entrenching his control is a different order of request, and it's already drawing attention from political figures wary of Musk's influence, Ars Technica noted.

Regulatory scrutiny is the second. Combining a major U.S. defense and space contractor with a publicly traded automaker and energy company would likely draw attention from antitrust regulators and national-security reviewers given SpaceX's government contract base, even if the two businesses don't directly compete. And the market's own reaction on Tuesday was telling: Tesla barely moved while SpaceX jumped, suggesting investors see this primarily as a referendum on which company captures more upside from AI and robotics — not a straightforward synergy story.

What to watch next

  • Committee progress reports — any public disclosure from Tesla's or SpaceX's independent directors about proposed terms, valuation methodology, or a timeline would be the first sign this is moving from study to negotiation.
  • Tesla's next shareholder meeting — Monday's proxy filing had no merger proposal; watch whether a special or annual meeting gets called specifically to address one, and how proxy advisory firms like ISS and Glass Lewis respond.
  • SpaceX's revenue trajectory — Musk's $500 billion to $1 trillion ARR projection for 2027 is aggressive even by his standards; whether SpaceX's compute-rental and Starlink/Starmind businesses actually scale that fast will shape how much leverage SpaceX brings to any exchange ratio.
  • Regulatory signaling — any early word from antitrust regulators or national-security reviewers about how they'd treat a SpaceX-Tesla combination, given SpaceX's extensive government contracts.
  • Musk's public framing — he's historically used earnings calls and X to shape shareholder sentiment well ahead of formal votes; expect more "superintelligence singularity"-style framing aimed at justifying the deal strategically rather than just financially.
Musk didn't announce a merger this week — he announced that the machinery to seriously consider one is now running, in public, for the first time. That alone is why this is the story every market desk and most of tech Twitter spent Tuesday afternoon arguing about.

Nothing here is finalized, and Musk went out of his way to say so. But the signal is unmistakable: two of the three most valuable companies Musk controls just told Wall Street, on the record, that combining into a single $3.5 trillion entity is no longer a hypothetical someone asks about on an earnings call — it's a live question with committees, filings, and a CEO openly rooting for yes.
