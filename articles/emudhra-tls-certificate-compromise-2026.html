8 Broken Keys, One Trillion Connections: Inside the eMudhra TLS Meltdown

October 22, 2026 · Kushagra · 8 min read

On the evening of October 17, a researcher known only as "Wayne" sent browser-security teams at Mozilla and Google something simple and terrifying: a short text message, digitally signed with the private key of a certificate authority trusted by every major browser on the planet. The message read, "We have a problem here — Wayne." It wasn't a hack in the traditional sense. Wayne hadn't stolen anything. He'd simply computed the key — because the key was never actually secret to begin with.

The bug: a 384-bit lock with a 4-bit key

The certificate authority is eMudhra Limited, an India-based CA that sells TLS and code-signing certificates worldwide under the "emSign" brand through entities in India, the United States, and the Netherlands. In April 2025, eMudhra generated eight intermediate CA certificates inside hardware security modules certified to FIPS 140-2 Level 3 — supposedly tamper-proof, audited, gold-standard hardware. Each certificate was supposed to carry a NIST P-384 elliptic-curve private key, which should have roughly 384 bits of entropy: a number with 116 digits, functionally unguessable.

Instead, researchers found the actual private keys were integers like 4, 56, 66, 115, 155, 156, 230, and 237. Not 384-bit numbers — single- and double-digit numbers. eMudhra has since admitted that a flaw in its key-wrapping ceremony generated a second, catastrophically weak set of keys alongside the properly generated ones, and that the weak set is what got published to the browser root stores by mistake. Security researcher Flüpke later posted one of the keys — value: 4 — publicly on GitHub, and BleepingComputer independently verified it matched a live, revoked eMudhra certificate.

The practical consequence: anyone who found this before Wayne did could have minted a browser-trusted TLS certificate for any domain on earth — your bank, Microsoft, Google — with zero need to compromise a server, zero phishing, zero malware. Just arithmetic.

Why this is a bigger deal than it sounds

An intermediate CA certificate sits between the root certificate (which your OS and browser ship with and implicitly trust) and the end-entity certificate your bank's web server presents to your browser. If you hold a valid intermediate's private key, you can sign a certificate for literally any hostname, and every browser that trusts that root will accept it without complaint. The six compromised certificates covering domain-validated, organization-validated, and extended-validation TLS, plus two for code signing, chain back to emSign ECC Root CA — G3, which was in the Chrome, Apple, and (until October 20) Mozilla root stores.

Cryptography engineer Filippo Valsorda, who maintains Go's standard-library crypto packages, didn't mince words to Ars Technica: "This will be a massive cleanup job, bigger than Heartbleed. There will be a very, very long tail — IoT devices, Docker containers, forgotten Linux machines, unmaintained CLI tools that shipped their own copy of the roots, which is common in some ecosystems, like Python." Browsers can push a revocation list in minutes. Everything else — embedded devices, air-gapped servers, abandoned firmware — has to be individually patched or rebuilt, and some of it never will be.

The response: fast, coordinated, and still incomplete

To their credit, the industry moved with unusual speed. Timeline, per Mozilla's own incident report and Chrome Root Program disclosures:

  • Oct 17, 20:06 UTC — Wayne's report reaches Mozilla via the Chrome Root Program.
  • Oct 17, 19:45 UTC (Chrome) / 22:59 UTC (Firefox) — both browsers push silent revocation-list updates (CRLSet and OneCRL respectively), blocking all eight certificates within hours, no user update or restart required.
  • Oct 18 — eMudhra formally revokes all eight certificates and halts new issuance under its G3 root.
  • Oct 19 — Apple blocks the certificates; Sectigo revokes its cross-signed copies (the eight certs were also cross-signed under Sectigo's AAA root, though both Chrome and Firefox had already distrusted that root for websites since April 2025, limiting the blast radius there); Android devices get an equivalent block via Google Play Services around 22:00 UTC.
  • Oct 20 — Microsoft blocks the certificates; eMudhra and Mozilla both publish technical write-ups; India's CERT-In issues advisory CIAD-2026-0050.
  • Oct 21 — eMudhra files a disclosure with the BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange), confirming the key-wrap ceremony flaw and stating it is still investigating root cause.

As of this writing, certificate transparency logs — the public ledgers that record every browser-trusted certificate ever issued — show no confirmed instance of the weak keys being used to mint a malicious certificate in the wild. That's the one piece of good news in an otherwise alarming story. Microsoft's own Edge browser on Windows 10 was, notably, still connecting to a dozen vulnerable hosts as late as Tuesday morning, and a company spokesperson would only say Microsoft would be in touch if it had anything to "share" — not exactly a reassuring posture from one of the three companies that controls the root-trust ecosystem.

Who's actually at risk right now

For the average person browsing in an up-to-date Chrome, Firefox, or Safari, the practical risk today is low — the blocks are in place, and exploiting a forged certificate still requires an attacker to physically sit on the network path between you and the server (a man-in-the-middle position), not just possess the key. The sharpest exposure is for VPN users, particularly in countries where governments have both the capability and incentive to intercept traffic at the network layer — exactly the adversary model this kind of flaw is tailor-made for. Apps that don't rely on TLS for confidentiality at all — Signal, WhatsApp, WireGuard — are unaffected by design, which is itself a quiet argument for end-to-end encryption that doesn't lean on the CA trust hierarchy.

The uncomfortable structural point

This isn't really a story about one careless company. It's a story about how fragile the entire TLS trust model is: it is only ever as strong as its weakest certificate authority, and there are hundreds of CAs baked into every major root store, each one a single point of failure for the whole internet. Certificate Transparency logging — introduced precisely to catch this kind of failure — worked exactly as designed here: it let researchers and browser vendors detect and distrust bad certificates quickly. But as several researchers pointed out, CT only helps once you're actively watching the feeds, and it does nothing for software that doesn't check revocation status at all, which describes an enormous share of the non-browser internet.

What to watch next

  • Root cause, confirmed — eMudhra says a flaw in its "key wrap and unwrap" ceremony step produced the weak keys; a fuller forensic account (and whether other eMudhra-issued certificates share the same flaw) is still pending.
  • The Linux/IoT long tail — watch for disclosures of actual exploitation months from now, surfacing from embedded devices and unmaintained software that never got patched.
  • CA/Browser Forum fallout — expect renewed pressure for stricter, audited key-ceremony requirements, and possibly formal distrust proceedings against eMudhra from Mozilla or Google, which have both pulled trust from CAs before (DigiNotar, Symantec) over far less severe failures.
  • Enterprise and government exposure — organizations relying on eMudhra-issued code-signing certificates for internal software need to check whether any of their signed binaries chain through the two compromised code-signing intermediates.
  • Edge/Windows 10 patch timing — Microsoft's slower response is worth tracking as a signal of how seriously legacy-platform vendors treat CA-level incidents versus mainstream browsers.
The headline risk isn't that someone stole a secret. It's that the "secret" was never generated correctly in the first place, inside hardware explicitly certified to prevent exactly that failure — and it sat trusted by billions of devices for eighteen months before anyone noticed.

Heartbleed, in 2014, exposed a bug in widely used software. This is arguably worse in kind, if not yet in scale: a foundational failure in the cryptographic ceremony that the entire web's trust model depends on, caught this time by a researcher acting in good faith — not by the people who were supposed to be certifying it was safe.
