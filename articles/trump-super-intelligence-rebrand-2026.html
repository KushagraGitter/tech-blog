Trump Wants to Ban the Word "AI" — and Corporate America Can't Decide Whether to Laugh or Comply

November 4, 2026 · Kushagra · 7 min read

Three words have dominated tech Twitter, earnings calls, and even Justice Department press releases this past week, and none of them are a model name, a chip shortage, or a breach disclosure. They're "Super Intelligence," capital S, capital I — President Trump's self-declared replacement for the term "artificial intelligence," which he now says he intends to officially ban. What started as a Truth Social poll in September has metastasized into a genuine corporate loyalty test, and the tech industry's reaction — equal parts groveling and open mockery — has become the most-discussed tech story of the week.

How we got here

On September 29, Trump hosted a lunch at the White House with AI industry leaders including Nvidia's Jensen Huang, Google's Sundar Pichai, Anthropic's Dario Amodei, and Meta's Mark Zuckerberg. That same day he signed an executive order titled "Inaugurating The Era Of Super Intelligence," directing every executive branch department and agency to drop "artificial intelligence" and "AI" from official correspondence, policy documents, and public communications in favor of "SI" or "super intelligence." The White House fact sheet framed it as recognizing "the continuously advancing technological frontier and the limitless promise it offers the American people."

The name itself came from a Truth Social poll Trump ran the week before, asking followers to pick between "SUPER INTELLIGENCE" and "SUPERIOR INTELLIGENCE." His reasoning, delivered at the UN General Assembly and repeated since: "The use of the word artificial makes intelligence sound fake. It is not fake. It's actually amazing." He has also claimed, without corroboration from Beijing, that Chinese President Xi Jinping endorsed the rebrand during a prior meeting.

Then, on October 27, Trump escalated from executive-branch directive to open threat. In a Truth Social post aimed at the Wall Street Journal for continuing to use "AI," he wrote that he would soon be "officially BANNING the term, 'AI/Artificial Intelligence,' in all relevant documents, and everything else, due to its inaccuracy." Days earlier he'd gone further still, declaring: "The White House considers anyone that uses the term, 'Artificial Intelligence,' as opposed to the highly accepted new and more accurate term, 'Super Intelligence,' THE ENEMY!"

Why it's blowing up right now

The story crossed from political curiosity to genuine tech-industry news this week because of earnings season. CNBC's analysis found that Microsoft, SpaceX, Meta, Tesla, and IBM all used "Super Intelligence" or "SI" in official filings and earnings calls during the last week of October — one SpaceX filing reportedly used "SI" 85 times. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella opened his shareholder letter in the company's annual report by declaring the arrival of "this Super Intelligence era." Elon Musk, who renamed his company's AI division from SpaceXAI to SpaceXSI in early October, told Tesla's earnings call that certain humanoid robot capabilities "aren't really using any kind of advanced AI or SI." Zuckerberg leaned into language Meta had already adopted with its "Meta Superintelligence Labs" unit, talking up "personal superintelligence for everyone." Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan blamed a chip shortage on "unprecedented demand for super intelligence," and Palo Alto Networks CEO Nikesh Arora called the White House meeting on SI "an epic moment" on X.

Not everyone jumped. Sam Altman called "Super Intelligence" a "more accurate" term in conversation but rejected renaming OpenAI to "OpenSI," telling Politico flatly: "I think people are going to keep calling it AI." IBM's Arvind Krishna used both terms interchangeably on his earnings call rather than fully switching. And the rebrand has already bled into government bureaucracy in ways that look faintly absurd: the U.S. Center for AI Standards and Innovation — itself a 2025 rebrand of the AI Safety Institute — has now been renamed the Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI), and a Justice Department press release describing a music-streaming fraud case now reads as "Super Intelligence-Assisted Music Streaming Fraud."

The backlash is louder than the compliance

Public reaction has run heavily negative. A recent Quinnipiac University poll found only 25% of voters satisfied with Trump's handling of AI policy, against 58% dissatisfied — numbers that undercut the stated rationale for the rebrand, which per Trump is partly about countering public anxiety over data centers and AI's economic disruption. Adam Kovacevich, CEO of the industry coalition Chamber of Progress, told Politico the compliance from CEOs "is a way... to signal fealty to the president," adding: "No one in the industry is about to start calling it superintelligence. It's like Gulf of America and Lake America. It's something that only MAGA supplicants use." Tech researcher Adam Thierer of the R Street Institute pointed out a more technical problem: "superintelligence" already has an established meaning in AI research — an AI system that surpasses human cognitive ability across domains, the scenario safety researchers worry about — and overloading that term to mean "AI" generally just muddies an already fraught policy debate. University of Chicago marketing professor Jean-Pierre Dube was blunter to AFP: "If we believe that 'artificial' already encourages fear, 'super' seems like something far more worrisome... a poor choice of new brand."

There's also a legal reality the rebrand can't paper over. Trump cannot compel Congress, the courts, or private companies to drop "artificial intelligence," and the term remains embedded in existing federal statute — including, awkwardly, Trump's own signature Take It Down Act, which administration lawyers will still have to cite by its legal name in court. Despite the bluster, no enforcement mechanism for the "ban" has been announced, and the Independent reported that outreach to Nvidia, Palantir, Meta, OpenAI, Google, Tesla, and Anthropic about their marketing plans went entirely unanswered.

Context: branding wars are a Trump signature

This isn't an isolated impulse. Trump has a long pattern of forcing nomenclature fights as a form of political signaling — from "Lake America" (his attempted renaming of Lake Ontario amid a Canada trade dispute) to "Gulf of America." What makes the AI rebrand different is that it's landing squarely on an industry Trump has otherwise tried to champion with minimal regulation, even as public trust in AI sits near record lows. The rebrand effectively asks the companies Trump has been courting to publicly launder his preferred vocabulary in exchange for continued access and goodwill — a trade several CEOs have now visibly made.

What to watch next

  • Q4 earnings season — whether more companies beyond the first wave (Microsoft, SpaceX, Meta, Tesla, Intel, IBM, Palo Alto Networks) adopt "SI" language, or whether the trend quietly fades once the news cycle moves on.
  • Any actual enforcement mechanism — Trump has threatened a "ban" with no specified penalty; watch for whether any executive order, procurement rule, or contract clause gives the threat real teeth beyond rhetoric.
  • Media and AP Stylebook holdouts — the Associated Press Stylebook still lists "artificial intelligence" as the standard term; a shift there would be the clearest signal the rebrand has actual linguistic traction outside politics.
  • Collision with existing AI-safety vocabulary — "superintelligence" already means something specific and alarming to AI researchers (a system surpassing human ability broadly); watch whether confusion between the political rebrand and the technical term complicates the separate policy fight over frontier-AI regulation, including Senators Sanders and Casar's "Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act," introduced the same month as Trump's order.

Whether "Super Intelligence" survives as anything more than an earnings-call tic or a Truth Social talking point is an open question. But for one week in late October and early November, the fight over what to call the technology reshaping the global economy generated more tech-world chatter than any actual AI product launch — which, depending on how cynically you read it, might be exactly the point.
