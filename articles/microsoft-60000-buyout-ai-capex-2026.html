Microsoft Offered Buyouts to a Quarter of Its Workforce — Then Reported Record Profit Two Days Later

October 29, 2026 · Kushagra · 8 min read

On Monday, October 26, Microsoft Chief People Officer Amy Coleman posted a memo titled "Introducing a Voluntary Exit Offer" to the company blog. It offered roughly 60,000 employees — about 27% of Microsoft's 223,000-person global workforce — a severance package to leave voluntarily by December 31. Two days later, on Wednesday, the company reported $92.3 billion in quarterly revenue, $36.6 billion in profit (up 32% year-over-year), and an Azure cloud division growing 48%. Microsoft's stock popped more than 5% on the news. Put those two facts next to each other and you get the single most-discussed tech story of the week: one of the most profitable companies on earth is simultaneously paying tens of thousands of people to leave and spending more money than ever before — on AI infrastructure, not on them.

What exactly was offered

The Voluntary Exit Offer (VEO) covers employees at "level 70 and below" — Microsoft's internal scale, where, per compensation database Levels.fyi, 65-67 corresponds to principal engineers and 68-70 to partner/distinguished level. In practice, that means everyone up through some vice presidents is eligible. It's open in just four countries — the United States, Canada, India, and the United Kingdom — and spans Windows, Xbox, Copilot, AI experiences, finance, and HR. Notably excluded, according to Bloomberg and a company spokesperson speaking to CBS News: data center teams, sales, and marketing — the groups most directly tied to the AI buildout and revenue generation. Employees have from the first week of December to accept; those who do will exit December 31. Workers at level 64 and below get one week of pay per six months of tenure; levels 65-67 get two weeks, plus six-to-12 months of continued stock vesting, temporary healthcare, and outplacement support, per Business Insider's reporting on the terms.

This is Microsoft's second voluntary exit program in 2026. In April it offered early retirement to about 7% of its U.S. workforce — roughly 8,700 people, mostly long-tenured senior staff — and saw a 30% acceptance rate. That earlier round is explicitly the model: Coleman's memo says the new VEO "builds on what we learned" from it. Layered on top: Microsoft cut 4,800 jobs in July (about 2.1% of global headcount, concentrated in Xbox, with up to five game studios divested), and cut more than 15,000 roles across all of 2025. The scale keeps climbing even as the company insists each round is "voluntary" and framed around employee choice rather than cost control.

Why it's landing as an AI story, not an HR story

Microsoft's own announcement never mentions AI or cost-cutting by name — Coleman's language is all "flexibility" and "shaping their next chapter." But the financial context makes the subtext impossible to ignore. Fiscal 2026 capital expenditures hit $115.95 billion, up nearly 80% year-over-year, driven almost entirely by AI data centers and chips, according to figures reported by Yahoo Finance. CFO Amy Hood told investors Microsoft spent $45.2 billion on capex in the September quarter alone — up roughly 30% from a year earlier — and guided to more than $50 billion in the next quarter, putting full-year 2026 spending near $170 billion. Three-quarters of that capex, Hood said, went to "short-lived assets" like memory chips and storage rather than buildings, meaning it depreciates fast and keeps recurring. Free cash flow, meanwhile, fell 28% year-over-year to $18.6 billion even with revenue and profit both up sharply — the clearest sign that AI infrastructure spending is outrunning the cash the business throws off, even at Microsoft's scale.

Payroll is one of the few costs management can move quickly; a data center lease signed through the 2030s is not. That's the calculus analysts keep pointing to: Microsoft can't easily slow its roughly $20 billion compute deal with Nebius or its CoreWeave capacity commitments, so headcount becomes the lever. Brian Mulberry, chief market strategist at Zacks Investment Management, put the mood on Wall Street bluntly: "I don't think we're out of patience. But I do think people are paying attention to it now, in a way that they haven't been before."

The timing: earnings beat, stock pop, buybacks continue

The October 28 earnings call gave Microsoft a strong hand to make this move from. Azure grew 48% — "far outpacing the increase in capex," per Microsoft's own earnings deck, which cited "timing differences between receipt of goods and payment and a lower mix of finance leases." CEO Satya Nadella told investors Microsoft added 24 new data centers globally last quarter. Results beat Wall Street's expectations on both revenue ($92.3B vs. $90.6B expected) and profit, and shares rose more than 4-5% in the aftermath — a sharp contrast to the same week's "disappointing" results from Meta and Alphabet, both also deep in AI capex cycles, as The Straits Times noted. That contrast is doing a lot of the narrative work here: Microsoft is positioning its AI bet as the one that's actually paying off, using a strong quarter to justify both record spending and a workforce reduction in the same breath.

It's also happening amid broader leadership churn. Ryan Roslansky, an executive vice president and former LinkedIn CEO, recently announced his departure, as did Chief Communications Officer Frank X. Shaw — part of what Fortune described as "a wave of executive departures" as Nadella reshapes Microsoft's leadership structure to chase enterprise and cloud AI dominance.

Why this hit differently than past Microsoft layoff news

Microsoft has cut jobs before without becoming the week's top tech story. What's different this time is the juxtaposition of numbers happening within 48 hours: a buyout offer covering more people than most Fortune 500 companies employ in total, immediately followed by a profit report that proves the company has more cash than it knows what to do with — it's simply choosing to spend that cash on silicon and data centers instead of people. Historically, 3-5% of eligible employees take a given buyout offer, per Deadline's reporting on broader industry patterns, and Microsoft's own April program saw 30% uptake specifically because of self-selection: it was aimed narrowly at long-tenured staff weighing retirement anyway. This round is far broader in scope — specifically because it's not just older staff being targeted — which is part of why it's generating so much more discussion than the April program did.

What to watch next

  • Actual uptake numbers — Microsoft will disclose by early January how many of the 60,000 eligible employees actually accepted; a low take rate (closer to the industry's 3-5% norm) would suggest this is more a signaling exercise than a real cost-cutting mechanism, while a high rate would validate the April precedent repeating at scale.
  • Whether declines turn into involuntary cuts — voluntary programs are often followed by mandatory layoffs for teams that don't hit target headcount reductions organically; watch Xbox, Windows, and Copilot specifically.
  • Q2 capex guidance — Hood already flagged more than $50 billion in the current quarter; whether that number rises again, and whether Microsoft can finally quantify savings from the VEO on the next earnings call, will tell investors if the payroll-for-capex trade is actually working.
  • Peer moves — with Meta and Alphabet posting weaker AI-capex optics the same week, watch whether Google, Amazon, or Meta follow with their own white-collar buyout programs as the "fund AI by cutting headcount" playbook spreads across Big Tech.
Microsoft just proved, in the space of one week, that a company can be making more money than ever and still decide a quarter of its workforce is a cost it would rather not carry into the AI era.

The official line is choice and flexibility. The financial filings tell a simpler story: $170 billion a year is going into chips and data centers, free cash flow is shrinking even as profit grows, and the easiest number left to move is headcount.
