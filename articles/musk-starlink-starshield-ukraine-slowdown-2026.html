Musk, Not Trump, Ordered the Starlink Slowdown That Blindsided Ukraine

October 28, 2026 · Kushagra · 8 min read

For ten days starting October 14, Ukrainian drone pilots trying to shoot down Russian Shahed attack drones kept losing their video feed the instant they flew faster than 100 miles an hour. Nobody told them why. It took a Wall Street Journal investigation, a string of contradictory denials from the two most powerful men in the room, and a dead civilian count to get an answer: Elon Musk ordered it himself, and then spent a week letting the world think it might have been Donald Trump's call instead.

This is now the story dominating tech and foreign-policy coverage simultaneously — Ars Technica, CNBC, Reuters, the Associated Press, and the WSJ itself have all run follow-ups in the last 72 hours, and it's pulling in everyone from Senator John Cornyn to UC Berkeley defense researchers. The reason it's blowing up isn't just the war angle. It's that a privately owned satellite network has become so load-bearing for a NATO-backed war effort that one CEO's unilateral decision can measurably change who lives and who doesn't on a given night.

What actually happened

Starshield is the military-grade version of SpaceX's Starlink service, operated under a U.S. government contract that, according to a source who spoke to a competing outlet, runs through May 15, 2027. Early on October 14, without advance warning, Ukrainian units found that any Starshield terminal moving faster than roughly 160 km/h (100 mph) — the normal operating speed for Ukraine's Shahed-intercepting drones — lost its data connection entirely. The restriction wasn't theoretical: Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy wrote on X that equipment was cutting out at speeds as low as 130–161 km/h "in various sectors of the front." The Guardian reported two deaths and at least 17 injuries from Russian strikes on energy infrastructure during the window the restriction was active, and the WSJ documented a specific incident in which a six-drone strike on a Russian warehouse lost contact with several aircraft mid-mission after the speed cap kicked in.

Musk's first public response, on October 14, was categorical: "Starshield systems are owned and controlled by the US government. Any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS." Trump backed that framing days later, telling reporters "I didn't do that... I heard about it through the news." But a Reuters report dated October 15 said the order "came from inside the White House," while a Telegraph report said Musk had suggested the slowdown to Trump, who then approved it. Then, on October 20, the Journal's follow-up collapsed the ambiguity: citing U.S. officials and people familiar with the matter, it reported Musk made the final call himself, timed to land just after both he and Trump had publicly criticized Ukraine for striking Russian diesel refineries. Starshield access was "working more normally" again shortly after Trump personally called Musk on October 22 and asked him to lift it, per CNBC's sourcing.

Nobody agrees on why — or whether it's even possible

Musk later offered a second, different justification on X: that Ukraine had been exploiting a "loophole" to run offensive strikes on the civilian Starlink network, which he said was never meant for lethal use, and that the loophole had simply been "closed." Ukrainian technical sources immediately disputed that this was even feasible. Serhii Sternenko, a former drone adviser to Ukraine's Ministry of Defense who helped negotiate anti-abuse terminal whitelisting with SpaceX earlier this year, told Telegram (per Business Insider) that "as someone who was inside the process... I can say for sure that this is not true." Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov, a defense-technology adviser to Zelenskyy, said civilian Starlink terminals are capped at roughly 90 km/h and geofenced out of Russian territory — meaning the high-speed interceptor and strike drones Musk was describing have never run on the civilian service at all; they run exclusively on Starshield, the system he admits he personally restricted.

That contradiction is the crux of why this story has legs beyond a single news cycle: Musk's own explanations don't agree with each other, let alone with Ukraine's engineers.

Why this is landing as more than a war story

The reaction in Washington has been unusually bipartisan. Senator John Cornyn (R-TX) said on CNBC's Squawk Box he didn't believe Musk acted "without at least the acquiescence of the White House," and separately called attempts to limit Starlink "shameful." Representative Pramila Jayapal (D-WA) went further, demanding an investigation and arguing "no billionaire... should have the power to unilaterally cut off communications and weapons in wartime." Robert Weissman of the advocacy group Public Citizen called it "extreme danger," saying "no one man should have that much power."

That framing — one man, one company, controlling a capability that functions as de facto national military infrastructure for an ally at war — is what tech and policy writers are converging on. Andrew Reddie, a defense researcher at UC Berkeley, told The Verge the situation resembles "an antitrust issue" because SpaceX both launches and owns the Starlink constellation, leaving no competing provider for a country like Ukraine to fall back on. "You've effectively got a non-state actor making decisions as if they're a government," he said. It's also not the first time: Starlink coverage was reportedly withheld from a 2022 Ukrainian counterattack near Crimea, and Musk has previously objected to the U.S. military routing suicide-drone operations over the civilian Starlink network, arguing it violated SpaceX's own usage terms.

The business and political backdrop

The episode lands at an already tense moment for the Musk-Trump-Ukraine triangle: both men have publicly blamed Kyiv for driving up diesel prices by striking Russian oil refineries, and the throttling began within days of those comments. It also lands while SpaceX and Musk's other ventures are under heavy scrutiny already — Bloomberg reported this week that SpaceX and Tesla's boards are studying a possible $3.5 trillion merger, a headline that, taken together with the Starshield story, reinforces a single narrative: Musk's commercial empire and U.S. government capability are becoming difficult to cleanly separate, whether in war-fighting infrastructure or corporate structure.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio tried to draw a hard line for the administration, telling reporters "the president has nothing to do with the decisions that are made by Starlink and Starshield and by Mr. Musk. That's a private company." But that statement sits awkwardly next to CBS's reporting that Musk held "multiple conversations with administration officials leading up to the order" and "wanted clarity on the president's thinking on the matter" — which reads less like total private-sector independence and more like a company CEO seeking political cover before making a call that would affect a live war.

What to watch next

  • Congressional follow-through — Jayapal's call for an investigation is the first concrete legislative response; watch whether it gets a committee hearing or stays a press statement.
  • Contract language — Ukraine's Starshield arrangement reportedly runs through May 2027; any public disclosure of what operational authority that contract actually grants SpaceX versus the Pentagon would resolve the "who's really in charge" question better than any official's quote.
  • Whether restrictions return — service was restored after Trump's October 22 call to Musk, but Zelenskyy said as of this week the limits "have not been completely lifted" for all units, so this isn't fully closed.
  • The antitrust/dependency angle — expect renewed debate (echoed in Moneywise's reporting) about whether the Pentagon should reduce reliance on a single commercial satellite provider for frontline military connectivity.
  • Musk's shifting explanations — he has now offered two different justifications (military/POTUS decision; closed "loophole") that both appear to be contradicted by his own prior statements or by Ukrainian technical sources. A third version, or a retraction, would be newsworthy on its own.
The through-line isn't really about drones or diesel refineries. It's that a war-fighting capability NATO's most exposed member depends on for survival can be switched on and off by one unelected executive, on a timeline nobody else in government appears to control or fully understand.
