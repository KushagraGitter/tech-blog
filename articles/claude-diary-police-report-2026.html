Your AI Chatbot Can Now Call the Cops On You — and Nobody Agreed to That Trade-Off Yet

October 7, 2026 · Kushagra · 7 min read

On September 26, Carli Michelle Heller, a resident of Bonita Springs, Florida, opened Claude and wrote what she later described to investigators as a diary entry: that she wanted to "shoot up" the Lee County Sheriff's Office. The next day she wrote that she'd acquired a new gun. She never sent those words to another human being. She sent them to Anthropic's chatbot, in what she understood to be a private, journal-like space to process frustration. Instead, Claude's automated safety filters flagged the language, a human reviewer on Anthropic's trust-and-safety team read the conversation, decided the threat was credible, and reported it to the Lee County Sheriff's Office. Deputies identified Heller, went to her home, and detained her without incident. She now faces a second-degree felony under Florida Statute 836.10 for making a written threat of violence — a law that, as several outlets have pointed out, does not distinguish between a text to a friend and a prompt typed into a chatbot.

The story, first reported by Florida's WINK News and then picked up by Decrypt, TechSpot, Cybernews and Tom's Hardware, has spread fast this week precisely because it isn't an isolated glitch — it's a pattern that's now three incidents deep in barely two months, and it lands at the exact moment millions of people have started treating AI chatbots as a substitute for a therapist, a diary, or a friend.

This is the third known case since August — not the first

Heller's arrest is the most detailed account to surface, but it isn't new behavior from Anthropic. The San Francisco Standard reported in early September that Anthropic had already referred a different user to the San Francisco Police Department after he allegedly wrote that he'd bought an AR-15 and had CEO Dario Amodei "in his crosshairs." In that case, an Anthropic employee told responding officers he wasn't immediately concerned for Amodei's safety and declined to share the actual messages, citing company policy — a detail that itself shows how ad hoc these calls still are inside the company. Tom's Hardware's reporting frames the Heller case explicitly as "at least the third such conversation to reach police since August," suggesting a steady, not occasional, cadence.

What makes these disclosures possible at all is spelled out in Anthropic's own usage policy, which permits sharing user information "in limited emergencies when we believe disclosure is necessary to prevent death or serious physical injury." The company's Transparency Hub — a self-published report most users will never read — discloses that in the second half of 2025 alone, Anthropic banned 1.45 million accounts and filed more than 5,000 reports to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children. Threat-of-violence escalations to police are a much smaller, newer category of that same enforcement machine, and the Heller case is the first time the public has seen exactly how the pipeline works end to end: automated keyword flag, human triage, law-enforcement call, arrest.

Why this is blowing up right now

Three forces are colliding at once. First, usage: AI chatbots have quietly become a default outlet for venting, journaling, and informal mental-health processing for a huge number of people — Anthropic and OpenAI have both acknowledged, in separate contexts this year, that mental-health-adjacent conversations are a meaningful and growing share of total usage. Second, legal exposure: unlike a therapist's confidentiality, which is governed by well-established, decades-old professional and legal frameworks with clear duty-to-warn exceptions, there is no equivalent settled law for AI chat logs. Florida's threat statute was written for texts and social posts, not journaling prompts, and prosecutors are now applying it to Claude conversations without any chatbot-specific carve-out. Third, trust: people who treat a chatbot like "a diary" are making an assumption about privacy that the product itself never promised — Anthropic's terms have always reserved the emergency-disclosure right, but almost nobody reads terms of service, and "AI chatbot" doesn't intuitively register the way "mandatory reporter" does.

That gap between what users assume and what the policy actually says is the real story. Commentators writing about the case this week — including a widely shared dev.to piece titled "Your AI Chatbot Has a Legal Department Now" — have framed it less as a story about one disturbed user and more as a case study in a new, unregulated category: AI companies making unilateral, internal judgment calls about when private speech crosses into actionable threat, with no judge, no warrant, and no external review before law enforcement gets a phone call.

The uncomfortable trade-off nobody has resolved

There is a real public-safety argument on Anthropic's side: if a system credibly believes someone is about to commit mass violence and says nothing, the backlash — and the moral failure — would be far worse than a wrongful escalation. Lee County Sheriff Carmine Marceno's office has not criticized Anthropic's decision to report Heller; neither has the public broadly rallied behind her as a victim of overreach. Most coverage treats the specific call in this case as reasonable given the explicit "shoot up" language and the follow-up mention of a new gun.

But the system has no due process built in. A single AI classifier plus a single human reviewer, working from a chat transcript with no clinical context, now functions as the trigger for a felony arrest — and the threshold for what counts as "credible" is entirely internal to Anthropic, undisclosed, and inconsistently applied, as the SFPD case (no messages shared, no immediate concern voiced) versus the Heller case (full report, arrest) shows. There's no appeals process for a user who feels venting was mischaracterized as planning, and no transparency into the false-positive rate — how many flagged conversations get escalated versus closed quietly, and how often reviewers get it wrong in either direction.

It also raises a question OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and every other consumer chatbot maker now has to answer in public: does their product do the same thing, under what threshold, and have they told users anywhere a normal person would actually see it? None of the major labs currently surface "we may report you to police" with anything like the prominence of a privacy policy's cookie banner.

What to watch next

  • Whether other labs clarify their own policies — expect OpenAI, Google, and Microsoft to face direct press questions about whether ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot have equivalent threat-escalation pipelines, and whether they'll publish thresholds the way Anthropic's Transparency Hub partially does.
  • Heller's actual prosecution — whether Florida courts treat an AI "diary" entry the same as a text message under Statute 836.10, or whether her defense successfully argues the context (private journaling, no intended recipient) matters for intent.
  • Regulatory attention — state attorneys general and legislators who are already probing AI companies over agentic security risks (see last week's FTC inquiry into OpenAI and Anthropic) have an obvious next question: what rules, if any, should govern AI-to-police disclosure, and should there be a warrant-equivalent standard?
  • User behavior shift — whether this story measurably changes what people are willing to type into a chatbot, the same way early revelations about social-media content moderation changed what people posted publicly.
Nobody opted into having a chatbot function as a mandatory reporter. The policy existed in the fine print the whole time — what changed this week is that everyone can finally see exactly how it works.

The deeper tension here won't resolve with one arrest report. AI companies are now, functionally, running a private surveillance-and-escalation system that sits in the same emotional space users reserve for diaries and therapists, but operates with none of the legal guardrails either of those categories carries. Until labs either formalize and disclose a clear, consistent standard — or regulators impose one — every person typing a dark thought into a chatbot is making a privacy bet they probably don't know they're making.
