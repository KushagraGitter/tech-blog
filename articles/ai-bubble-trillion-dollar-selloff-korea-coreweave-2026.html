The AI Trade Just Lost $1 Trillion in a Week — and Nobody Agrees on Why

October 16, 2026 · Kushagra · 8 min read

For two years, the AI trade only moved in one direction. This week it didn't. Twenty of the world's most valuable AI and semiconductor names shed more than $1 trillion in combined market value, according to CNBC's tally, even as the underlying business — chip demand — kept setting records. The disconnect between "the technology is selling more than ever" and "the stocks are getting crushed" is exactly why this is now the single most-discussed story in tech: it's the first time the AI boom's financing, rather than its capability, is the thing making headlines.

Korea tripped a circuit breaker. That almost never happens.

The clearest single data point came out of Seoul on October 15 — a session Korean trade press is already calling "Black Thursday." The KOSPI closed at 5,554.22, down 7.57% (454.59 points), triggering a sell-side circuit breaker and halting both the KOSPI and KOSDAQ simultaneously for the first time in two and a half months. Foreign investors net-sold roughly ₩3.63 trillion that single day; their cumulative net selling for 2026 now stands at ₩208.5 trillion, with 89% of it concentrated in just two stocks — Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix. Samsung fell 9.48% and SK Hynix 11.06%; Samsung's foreign-ownership ratio dropped to its lowest level in 18 years. Analysts at outlets tracking the move flagged a specific structural trigger: Samsung's buyback program ended October 6, and SK Hynix's ₩40 trillion buyback is scheduled to wrap around October 20 — meaning the two biggest buyers of these stocks are both stepping away from the market at once.

CoreWeave is the epicenter

If one company's balance sheet captures the whole story, it's CoreWeave, the Nvidia-backed "neocloud" that leases out GPU capacity. Its stock fell as much as 15.5% in a single session this week, and its debt is sending a louder signal than its equity: the yield to maturity on CoreWeave's 9.75% bond due 2031 climbed to 15.78%, and the spread on its five-year credit default swaps — insurance against the company defaulting — spiked above 1,140 basis points at one point. Gavan Nolan, executive director of fixed-income pricing at S&P Global Market Intelligence, noted that a spread above 1,000 basis points is generally read as a sign of credit distress. CoreWeave has borrowed heavily to buy Nvidia chips ahead of demand it's betting will materialize; the market, this week, started pricing in the possibility it won't materialize fast enough to service that debt.

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang pushed back directly, telling Bloomberg "CoreWeave's strength is improving every day... I'm optimistic. Their cost of capital is actually decreasing" — comments that helped CoreWeave's stock claw back close to 6% the next day. SPI Asset Management's Stephen Innes summed up the whiplash memorably: "AI Jesus had entered the trading room and, for a while at least, the machines found religion again."

The broader sell-off, in numbers

The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX) fell for six consecutive sessions, its longest losing streak in 11 years by some measures, closing at 11,684.38 (-4.3% on the day, down more than 10% across the run). Nvidia pulled back from its all-time high of $243 to the $212 range; AMD fell 5.3%, Micron 4.79%, Intel 5.34%, Broadcom 4.35%, Dell Technologies 6.8%, and Arm 6.48%. Optical-networking names, which ride the same AI-infrastructure wave, fell even harder — Coherent dropped 9.19% and Applied Optoelectronics 11.82%.

What makes this genuinely strange is that it's happening alongside record fundamentals. TSMC reported its sixth consecutive quarter of record profit on October 15: Q3 revenue of NT$1.4943 trillion (about $46.7 billion, up 50.9% year over year) and net profit of NT$763.1 billion, up 68.7%. CEO C.C. Wei said AI chip demand still exceeds supply. TSMC's ADR fell anyway — down about 4% the same day — because, as Saxo Bank's Charu Chanana put it, the market isn't contesting demand, it's contesting financing. Baird's Ross Mayfield argued the move is fundamentally about rates, not AI itself: September U.S. CPI came in hotter than expected, and producer prices then climbed 6.3% year over year — the fastest PPI growth since December 2022 — pushing up the odds of another Fed rate hike and making every dollar of AI-infrastructure debt more expensive to carry.

Why it's landing now, specifically

Three warnings converged in the same week. Research firm Gartner forecast that up to $3.5 trillion in current AI data-center capital expenditure could face impairment by 2030, predicting that as many as 80% of "neocloud" GPU-leasing providers like CoreWeave could cease to exist by then — calling the funding model, not the technology, "unsustainable." Separately, Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan and Broadcom CEO Hock Tan both said in Singapore that capital — not chip supply or engineering talent — is shaping up to be AI's real bottleneck, a notable admission from two chip executives who'd normally talk up demand rather than flag financing risk. And Anthropic disclosed Q3 revenue of $16.8 billion, up roughly 45–46% year over year, while pursuing a prospective IPO valuation near $2 trillion — a number one Wall Street analyst called "the most ridiculous IPO" of the year, arguing the figure overshoots a more defensible ~$150 billion valuation by as much as 93%.

Layer on top of that a separate, stranger thread: Anthropic published research warning about theoretical self-replicating "AI worms" that could spread across IoT devices, which drew immediate public pushback from White House AI and crypto advisor David Sacks questioning the framing — commentary elsewhere noted this fits a pattern of safety-focused labs issuing dramatic warnings that outpace demonstrated real-world risk, even as security researchers treated the underlying technical finding as worth monitoring.

The rebound — and the unresolved question

By Friday, Asian markets were bouncing: Seoul and Tokyo posted healthy early gains, led by SK Hynix and Kioxia, the same names hit hardest the day before. Traders were watching for comments from Fed chair nominee Kevin Warsh at the IMF–World Bank annual meetings in Bangkok, hoping for signal on whether this week's inflation data locks in a near-term rate hike. Historically, the pattern favors a bounce: of 39 times since 1994 the SOX fell six days in a row, it rebounded on day seven in 24 of them — but as one analyst tracking the Korean sell-off put it, the question isn't "why did it fall," it's "when do foreigners come back," and the likeliest catalysts (U.S. midterms, Anthropic's IPO, Samsung and SK Hynix's late-October earnings) are all still weeks away.

What to watch next

  • CoreWeave's actual refinancing — does Huang's confidence translate into lower borrowing costs, or does the 15%+ bond yield stick and start pressuring other GPU-leasing "neoclouds" the same way?
  • SK Hynix's ₩40 trillion buyback expiry around October 20 — Korea's single biggest near-term support for its AI-exposed stocks disappears right around this date.
  • Anthropic's IPO process — whether institutional investors actually underwrite a ~$2 trillion valuation, or whether the number quietly comes down before any listing.
  • Whether the Fed hikes again this month — the AI sell-off and the inflation story are now mechanically linked; a pause in rate hikes would remove one of the two pressures hitting AI-infrastructure debt at once.
  • Gartner's $3.5 trillion number — watch whether other analysts converge on it or dispute it; it's the first concrete, dated figure attached to "AI capex write-down risk" rather than vague bubble talk.
The AI technology story and the AI financing story have quietly split into two different narratives this week — chip demand has never been higher, and the debt used to build capacity to meet that demand has never looked riskier at the same time.

Nobody on either side is claiming the AI boom is over. What changed this week is that, for the first time, the people financing it — bond markets, credit-rating desks, Korean pension-fund-adjacent retail money — started pricing it like a normal, riskable bet rather than a one-way trade. That's a genuinely new phase, and it's why this story, not another model launch or benchmark chart, is the one actually moving markets and headlines together right now.
