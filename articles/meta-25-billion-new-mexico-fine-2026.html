Meta's $25 Billion Day: How One State That Refused to Settle Just Handed Down the Biggest Fine in Company History

October 26, 2026 · Kushagra · 8 min read

On Wednesday, a state court judge in Santa Fe did something no regulator, no FTC settlement, and no congressional hearing has managed in over a decade of "techlash": he put a number on Meta's lying that actually hurts. US District Court Judge Francis J. Mathew ordered Meta Platforms to pay $25 billion to the state of New Mexico — the largest single-state penalty against a corporation in American history, and by a wide margin the largest fine Meta has ever faced. The story is dominating Ars Technica, Bloomberg Law, Variety, and PBS NewsHour simultaneously, for a simple reason: it's the first time a Cambridge Analytica-era accountability case has actually landed a blow big enough to move the conversation past "cost of doing business."

The math: from $219 billion down to $25 billion

The number everyone's repeating — $25 billion — is itself a discount. A New Mexico jury found in September that Meta had violated the state's Unfair Practices Act nearly 44 million times, stemming from deceptive statements about how Facebook handled user data, moderated hate speech and misinformation, and protected privacy in the years following the 2016 Cambridge Analytica scandal. At the statute's maximum penalty of $5,000 per violation, that total works out to a staggering $219.4 billion. Judge Mathew exercised discretion to bring it down to $25 billion — still roughly seven times what New Mexico's attorneys general actually asked a jury to find viable, and an amount Attorney General Raúl Torrez's office is calling "one of the single largest, if not the largest, single-state verdict against a company in American history."

For scale: New Mexico's entire projected general-fund revenue for fiscal 2028 is about $14.8 billion. The fine against one company, for violations tied to one state's 2 million residents, is larger than that state's whole annual budget.

Why New Mexico, and why now

The case traces back to the 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal, when a political consulting firm harvested data from roughly 87 million Facebook profiles to build targeted political advertising for the 2016 election. New Mexico sued in 2021, arguing Facebook had explicitly promised users control over their data while enabling exactly that kind of harvesting behind the scenes.

What makes New Mexico's case different is that it's the only state that didn't settle. In August 2026, Meta reached an $18 billion agreement with attorneys general from nearly all other states and Florida over related child-safety and social-media-addiction claims — a deal that would have released Meta from further liability tied to the Cambridge Analytica breach. New Mexico walked away from that table. Torrez argued the state's roughly 2 million residents were harmed and that nothing short of a penalty large enough to move Meta's stock price would force real change. The gamble paid off: New Mexico alone now holds a judgment bigger than the 49-state settlement combined.

Judge Mathew's written opinion didn't mince words about why he went to the top of the allowable range. The trial record, he wrote, showed that Meta's top leadership — explicitly including Mark Zuckerberg — knew the company was making misleading public statements about its "control of data, third-party data misuse, misinformation, hate speech, and equal enforcement." That's the detail driving most of the outrage in the coverage: this isn't framed as negligence, it's framed as knowing deception from the top down.

Meta's response — and why this isn't over

Meta has confirmed it will appeal, and legal experts quoted by Reuters think it has a real shot at getting the number reduced — the company's lawyers already argued at trial that any award above roughly $3.4 billion would be constitutionally excessive and unlikely to survive appellate review, pointing to due-process limits on punitive-style penalties. A Meta spokesperson added that the ruling, combined with New Mexico's enforcement posture generally, makes it "all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico" — a line several outlets have mocked as tone-deaf given the dollar figures involved.

Under the New Mexico Constitution, money collected through consumer-protection penalties flows into the state's education fund. Torrez leaned into that framing hard in his press conference, suggesting the state "may have paid for the construction of schools for the next 100 years" — while also acknowledging the money is tied up pending appeal and won't arrive any time soon.

Why this is landing differently than past Big Tech fines

Meta has absorbed plenty of regulatory punches before — a $5 billion FTC settlement in 2019, various EU privacy fines in the hundreds of millions to low billions — and largely shrugged them off as line items. What's different about the New Mexico case is threefold: it's a jury verdict (not a negotiated settlement Meta could help shape), it explicitly ties the deception to named top executives rather than "the company" in the abstract, and the dollar figure is finally large enough that analysts are debating real stock-price impact rather than treating it as noise. It's also a preview of what happens when a single state refuses to take a group discount — New Mexico's $25 billion dwarfs its per-capita share of the 49-state, $18 billion deal by orders of magnitude, which is likely to embolden other attorneys general to hold out in future multistate negotiations rather than settle early.

What to watch next

  • The appeal. Meta's constitutional excessive-fines argument is a real legal avenue, and several legal experts think the number could shrink substantially on review — watch whether it survives anywhere near $25 billion or gets knocked down toward Meta's preferred $3.4 billion ceiling.
  • Copycat litigation. Florida also declined the $18 billion settlement; watch whether Florida's AG now pursues its own standalone trial using New Mexico's verdict as a template.
  • Market reaction. Does this actually move Meta's stock in a way smaller fines never did, validating Torrez's stated theory that only penalties tied to share price change corporate behavior?
  • Precedent for AI-era data claims. Expect state AGs to study this playbook for future suits over AI training data and consent, not just social-media-era privacy claims.
New Mexico didn't just win a bigger number than everyone else who settled — it proved that refusing the group discount can be the more profitable move, for a state and for the broader push to make Big Tech penalties actually sting.

Whether $25 billion survives appeal or gets cut down to a fraction of that, the verdict itself has already done its job: it reframed, for the first time in years, what a "real" tech accountability penalty is supposed to look like.
