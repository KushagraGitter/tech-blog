The AI Trade's Second Crack: Chips Fall 8 Days Straight, Anthropic's IPO Shrinks by $500 Billion

October 18, 2026 · Kushagra · 8 min read

A week ago, this blog covered the AI trade losing a trillion dollars over seven days. On October 16, it lost more than a trillion dollars in a single session — and the chip sector's slide has now stretched into territory nobody under 50 has traded through. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX) fell for an eighth consecutive day on Friday, its longest losing streak since October 2008, during the depths of the global financial crisis. At the same time, word leaked that some of Anthropic's own prospective IPO investors are now pushing to price the company at $1.5 trillion instead of the $2 trillion figure that was floating around just weeks ago. Two different signals, one shared conclusion: the market is starting to ask the AI boom to show its receipts.

The number: eight days, 15.7%, and a trillion dollars gone in one afternoon

The SOX dropped 3.83% to 11,137.29 on Friday, October 16 — its eighth straight losing session, something that hasn't happened since an eight-day stretch running October 1–10, 2008, per Yahoo Finance data going back to 1994. Over those eight sessions (Oct. 7–16) the index shed 15.7%, and it now sits 23.9% below its June 22 record close of 14,634.72, even though it's still up 57.2% for the year. All 30 constituents fell that day. Broadcom, Micron, AMD, Intel, Lam Research and KLA each dropped roughly 5%; Arm Holdings slid about 7%. The broader market followed: the Nasdaq 100 fell 2.5% to 29,187.55, the S&P 500 lost 1.1%, and more than $1 trillion in tech-stock value evaporated in that single trading day, according to 247wallst.com and CNBC's Asia desk reporting on the global spillover.

The pain traveled fast. In South Korea, SK Hynix fell more than 10% and Samsung Electronics dropped 8.73%. LG Innotek lost 8.95%, Seoul Semiconductor 9.28%, Samsung SDI 6.26%. Nvidia itself was comparatively unscathed — down just 1.43% to $214.13, holding a $5.17 trillion market cap — which tells you this selloff isn't (yet) a referendum on whether AI chips work. It's a referendum on who's paying for the buildings full of them.

CoreWeave is the stock everyone is actually worried about

The clearest pressure point is CoreWeave, the Nvidia-backed "neocloud" that rents out GPU compute to AI labs and carries roughly $50 billion in debt. Its shares fell nearly 12% on Friday alone and about 25% over the week, while yields on its bonds surged past 15% — a level traders generally read as distress territory, not routine risk pricing. Adam Crisafulli of Vital Knowledge told CBS News the core issue is structural: neocloud operators are financing capital-intensive data-center buildouts with debt and equity rather than operating cash flow, and investors are finally doing the math on whether the revenue shows up before the bonds come due.

Layer onto that two macro shocks that hit in the same week: West Texas Intermediate crude touched $101.91 a barrel on October 14 — a five-month high — and the 10-year Treasury yield stood at 5.10% on October 15. Higher oil feeds inflation; hotter inflation data (also out that week) raises the odds the Fed hikes again; higher rates raise the discount rate used to value future profits, which hits richly-valued, cash-burning growth stocks hardest and first. That's the textbook mechanism, and it's exactly what played out across chip and neocloud names simultaneously.

Then Anthropic's own numbers added fuel

On Wednesday, October 14, Anthropic held an investor day in San Francisco ahead of its planned November IPO. The news should have been reassuring: third-quarter revenue jumped 45% quarter-over-quarter to $16.8 billion, and the company said it was profitable on an adjusted basis for the first time. Reuters reported that despite those headline numbers, some current and prospective investors left disappointed by the deceleration — revenue had more than doubled from Q1's $11.6 billion to Q2, making the 45% Q2-to-Q3 jump look like a growth slowdown by comparison. Multiple people familiar with the discussions told Reuters that some investors are now pushing for a roughly $1.5 trillion valuation for the November share sale, down from the approximately $2 trillion figure previously floated — a $500 billion haircut before the stock has even priced. Context matters here too: Anthropic reported a $42 billion loss for 2025 in its IPO prospectus, even while projecting the AI buildout will keep demanding more capital, not less.

That's a specific, uncomfortable data point landing in the middle of a market already nervous about AI financing: the most safety-focused, arguably most hyped frontier lab is having its own IPO math renegotiated downward in real time, by the same class of investors who are simultaneously dumping CoreWeave bonds.

Nvidia's Jensen Huang says this is noise, not signal

Touring Europe during the worst of it, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang gave reporters in Berlin a one-liner: "All of this is going to pass, and the reason for that is very simple." He argued AI's economic impact will be "transformative" across "every single industry and every single country" and that concerns will fade as that becomes undeniable. Notably, per 247wallst.com's reporting, Huang announced no new orders, no revised guidance, and named no new customers — his comments were pure conviction, not new evidence. Nvidia's own stock barely moved (down 1.43%, then up slightly after hours), which is either a vote of confidence from the market or simply a reflection that Nvidia sells the hardware regardless of who's left holding the financing risk.

Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives offered the bull case in similar terms to Briefs: higher Treasury yields are "a financing question, not a demand question," and he called the pullback a buying opportunity, while naming three actual warning signs to watch — hyperscaler capex cuts, scrapped contracts, or falling GPU rental rates. None of those three has happened yet. What has happened is a credit-market story (CoreWeave's bond yields), a macro story (oil and rates), and now a valuation story (Anthropic) all converging in the same ten days.

What to watch next

  • Anthropic's actual IPO price — if it prices meaningfully below $1.5 trillion in November, that becomes the clearest public marker yet of investor sentiment turning on frontier-lab valuations specifically, not just infrastructure debt.
  • CoreWeave's bond yields and any refinancing news — yields above 15% on a company this central to AI compute are a genuine stress signal; watch whether other neoclouds (Nebius, IREN) see similar credit deterioration.
  • Q3 hyperscaler earnings next week — Tesla, Meta, and others report as "earnings season shifts into high gear," per Reuters/LSEG data showing analysts expect quarterly profit growth above 30%, largely AI-driven. Any cut to capex guidance from Microsoft, Google, Amazon, or Meta would be the "demand signal" Dan Ives says actually matters, as opposed to a rate signal.
  • Oil and the Fed — crude above $100 and 10-year yields above 5% are doing real work here; a reversal on either would likely take pressure off chip valuations even if nothing about AI fundamentals changes.
  • China's memory ramp — reports that ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) made progress on denser memory architecture added to the selloff; a global memory oversupply by 2029 is now an explicit worry among investors, per CNBC.
Nothing in this week's numbers proves the AI buildout is wrong. What they prove is that the market has started pricing the financing of that buildout as a distinct, separate risk from the technology itself — and once investors start asking who's actually paying the bill, the bill becomes the story.
